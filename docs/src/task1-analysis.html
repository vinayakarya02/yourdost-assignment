Parents & online gaming survey

Parents aren’t anti-gaming. They’re anxious.

Five evidence-backed insights from 67 parents whose children play online games, with the exact calculations, what they mean and what YourDOST and parents can do with them.

1Research objective

Understand how parents perceive online gaming, what worries them, how they intervene, and how those worries relate to family life, to shape parent-facing wellbeing content and campaigns on YourDOST.

3Cleaning & validation

  • Routing check: all 33 ‘No, my child doesn’t play’ rows are blank after Q1. The form sent them straight to submit, so they are excluded rather than imputed.
  • Duplicates: 0 exact duplicates and 0 repeated timestamps.
  • Missing values: none in attitude, fear, role or impact grids. The rules grid has 1–3 blanks per item, so rule percentages use the answered base.
  • Inconsistency: 10 parents ticked ‘I have no rules whatsoever’ and at least one specific rule. A parent is counted as having rules if any specific rule is True.
  • Encoding: Likert −2…+2; fear and impact 0–3; True/False → 1/0. Indices are item means: fear (5 items), family impact (3), perceived benefit (4).
  • Not available: the form has no gaming-duration question, so no duration analysis is attempted.
Reproducible: python analysis/analysis.py writes survey_clean.csv and results.json. Every figure in this document and the infographic is generated from that file.

High fear by concern (share of 67 parents)

Unexpected pattern: fathers see more upside

Fathers agree more often that gaming can be beneficial: 54.2% (13/24) vs 30.2% (13/43) of mothers. The mean benefit index is +0.45 vs -0.21 (Mann-Whitney p = 0.006). Mothers report high addiction fear slightly more often (60.5% vs 50%).

5Interpretation

The picture is worry without hostility. Parents recognise both sides of gaming, but their fears concentrate on the things that feel out of their control: compulsive use and slipping grades. Fear and family friction move together. And while almost every parent believes in rules, households with rules report no less conflict than those without. What is associated with lower fear is understanding: knowing why the child plays. Parents who have played with their child (32/67) also average a lower fear index (2.14 vs 2.42 on a 0–3 scale), though that gap is only borderline (p = 0.059). Yet 52.2% (35/67) say they aren’t interested in learning about games, which is the gap to close.

Correlation, not causation. This is a cross-sectional, self-reported survey. We cannot say rules fail, or that understanding reduces fear. Calmer, more curious parents may simply be both less fearful and more engaged.

6Limitations & potential biases

  • Small sample: n=67; subgroup cells are small (e.g. no-rules n=13, adolescents’ parents n=10). Age-group differences are not reported as findings.
  • Convenience sample: skewed to women (43/67), Kolkata and Bangalore; not representative of Indian parents.
  • Timing: collected during COVID-19 restrictions (Dec 2020), when screen time and family stress were unusually high.
  • Self-report and social desirability: parents may over-report rules and under-report conflict.
  • Survey design: no measure of actual gaming time; a single ‘age group’ field for families with several children; some data-quality issues (e.g. 10 contradictory rule answers, 1 parent of a 0–3-year-old reporting gaming).
  • Multiple comparisons: several tests were run; p-values near .05 should be read as directional.

7Recommendations

For parents

  • Ask your child what they enjoy about their favourite game, then play it once together.
  • Agree a few rules with your child (timing, exams, spending) instead of announcing them.
  • Notice your own worry; anxious conversations tend to escalate.

For YourDOST content

  • A ‘Curiosity before control’ parent series: short videos on why kids play and how to talk about it.
  • Lead campaigns with addiction and study-balance, not violence.
  • A gaming-and-family self-reflection that routes high-worry parents to counselling.

For B2B programmes & research

  • Offer a parent-wellbeing module in employer campaigns (working parents were 28/67).
  • Re-run with gaming hours, child-level age and a larger, balanced sample.
  • Pilot a family-communication workshop and measure change in parent-reported conflict.
